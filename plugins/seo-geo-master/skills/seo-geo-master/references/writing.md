# Writing for pages

Rules for any text written for a site: articles, product copy, titles, meta descriptions, local pages. The reader comes first. Google says AI features need no special writing and no chunking of content (facts.md, 2026-07-10), so none of this is a hack: it is plain clarity that readers, AI answers and featured snippets all reward.

1. **Answer first.** The first sentence under each heading answers the question the heading asks. Background comes after the answer, never before.
2. **Specifics beat claims.** Every section carries something concrete: a number with its source, a named tool or material, a step, an example. Example: write "Oil the table every three months", not "Regular care is important".
3. **Sourced facts.** Trace every figure, date or price to its primary source. The owner's own data is the best source. Never invent statistics, quotes, studies, experts or reviews.
4. **Experience only the owner has.** When a section needs something only the owner can supply, write a placeholder and ask for it; never fill it in. Format: `[ADD: a photo of the finished table]`, `[ADD: the real delivery time]`, `[ADD: a customer's words about the repair]`.
5. **Protected fields.** The title, meta description and H1 stay true to the body. No price, year or claim the page does not support.
6. **Headings are questions buyers ask.** Use the brand's own words for what makes it different (quote the site, `brief.md`).
7. **Customer language.** When keyword tools show zero (common in Hebrew), use the words customers use in reviews, forums, groups and support messages. Say where each phrase came from.
8. **Editor pass before handing over.** Ask four things: does it answer faster than what ranks now; is there anything here a reader cannot get from the top results; is every claim sourced; which sentences sound generated. Then give the 5 to 10 most useful edits, each as the quoted sentence and its replacement:

       Heading: "What counts as a fast-loading page?"
       Now: "In today's world, speed is more important than ever, and users expect pages to load instantly."
       Edit: "By the Core Web Vitals standard, loading is good when the largest visible element appears within 2.5 seconds for at least 75% of page loads (web.dev)."

   The edit answers the heading in its first sentence, carries a number, and names where the number comes from.

9. **Slop patterns to cut.** One instance is fine; clusters are the problem.
   - Throat-clearing openers: "In today's world", "Let's dive in".
   - Emphasis crutches: "It's important to note".
   - Inflated words: delve, leverage, robust, seamless, unlock, landscape.
   - "Serves as" where "is" works.
   - The "not X, it's Y" formula used repeatedly.
   - A rhetorical question followed by its answer.
   - Lists of three by reflex.
   - Participle tails: "highlighting its importance".
   - Vague attribution: "experts say".
   - Stakes inflation.
   - A closing summary that repeats the article.
10. **No em dashes, ever.** This is an absolute rule, not part of the clusters above: a single em dash is already too many. Use a full stop, a comma or a colon.
11. **Fact-check AI-assisted drafts.** A person checks every claim before publishing, including titles, meta descriptions, structured data and alt text (Google, 2026-10-01).
12. **Hebrew.** Write naturally for the reader, not as a translation. Keep brand and product names consistent. Check right-to-left rendering of mixed Hebrew and English (numbers, units, brand names) in titles and snippets.
